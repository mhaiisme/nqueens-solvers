\begin{abstract}
The N-Queens problem asks for a placement of $N$ queens on an $N\times N$ board such that no two queens attack each other. We solve it with Boolean satisfiability (SAT) and compare SAT with two other exact paradigms: constraint programming (CP) and integer linear programming (ILP).
For SAT, we implement five at-most-one (AMO) encodings (pairwise, binary, sequential counter, commander, product), test four further encodings from the PySAT library, and run eight CDCL solvers, including CaDiCaL, Glucose, Kissat and CryptoMiniSat. We then compare the best SAT configuration with Google OR-Tools CP-SAT, IBM CPLEX CP Optimizer, CPLEX MIP and Gurobi.
All encodings are verified by counting all solutions for $N=2,\dots,9$, which match the known values (OEIS A000170).
Under a 30-second, single-thread budget we find that: (i) the \emph{commander} encoding is the most robust, solving $N=512$ in 11.4~s with CaDiCaL; (ii) a small number of clauses does not guarantee fast solving: sequential counter is compact but times out from $N=256$; (iii) the best SAT configuration reaches $N=1000$ in 27.7~s, further than CP-SAT ($N\le256$); and (iv) CPLEX CP is the fastest method where its community licence allows it to run ($N\le128$), while Gurobi and CPLEX MIP are limited by their licences to $N\le44$ and $N\le31$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Motivation.}
The N-Queens problem is a classical benchmark in artificial intelligence and operations research~\cite{bell2009,letavec2002}. A solution exists for every $N\ge4$ and can even be built directly in $O(N)$ time~\cite{bell2009}, so the problem is not hard in theory. However, it is a good \emph{controlled testbed}: the same simple constraints can be modelled in very different ways, and we can measure how each modelling choice affects performance.

\paragraph{Three paradigms.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SAT} writes the board as a propositional formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) and solves it with a conflict-driven clause-learning (CDCL) solver~\cite{handbook2009,marques2009}. Performance depends strongly on how the ``at most one queen per line'' constraint is encoded~\cite{frisch2010,sinz2005,klieber2007,chen2010}.
  \item \textbf{Constraint programming (CP)} keeps the constraint as a global \texttt{AllDifferent} constraint and uses a dedicated propagator~\cite{regin1994}.
  \item \textbf{Integer linear programming (ILP)} writes the constraints as linear (in)equalities over 0/1 variables and solves them by branch-and-bound/cut.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Research questions.} We structure the study around three questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How does the choice of AMO encoding affect the CNF size and the solving time?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How do different SAT solvers perform on the same encoding?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How does the best SAT configuration compare with CP (CP-SAT, CPLEX CP) and ILP (Gurobi, CPLEX MIP)?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We implement five AMO encodings and use four PySAT encodings, and verify all of them by exhaustive solution counting for $N=2,\dots,9$.
  \item We derive closed-form clause counts for the pairwise and sequential-counter encodings and confirm them against the implementation.
  \item We benchmark the encodings on eight CDCL solvers on boards up to $1024\times1024$.
  \item We compare SAT with CP-SAT, CPLEX CP, Gurobi and CPLEX MIP, and state clearly up to which $N$ each commercial solver is limited by its licence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
%% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Hardware and software.}
Ubuntu 24.04 virtual machine, 1 vCPU, 3~GB RAM, Python 3.12. Software: python-sat 1.9.dev15 (CaDiCaL 1.5.3 and 1.9.5, Glucose~4, MiniSat~2.2, MapleChrono, Lingeling, Kissat 4.0.4), pycryptosat 5.16, OR-Tools 9.15 (CP-SAT), Gurobi 13.0.3, CPLEX 22.2 and CP Optimizer 22.2.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Each run is a fresh OS process with a \textbf{30-second} wall-clock limit (model building + solving) and a 2.7~GB memory limit.
  \item \TO\ = time-out (over 30~s); \MO\ = memory-out; \NA\ = model refused by the licence.
  \item All solvers use \textbf{one thread}; runs are executed strictly one after another.
  \item Each configuration is run once. Small time differences between neighbouring cells should therefore not be over-interpreted.
  \item Metric: total time in seconds (encoding + loading + solving).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Licence limits.}
The commercial solvers are used with free community/evaluation licences, which limit the model size (Table~\ref{tab:licence}). A \NA\ entry is a licence limit, not a solver failure.

\subsection{RQ1b -- Solving time per encoding}
Table~\ref{tab:enc} and Fig.~\ref{fig:enc} give the total time with CaDiCaL 1.5.3.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small boards ($N\le32$):} every encoding finishes in under 1~s.
  \item \textbf{Commander} is the fastest or nearly fastest from $N=64$ on (0.20~s at $N=64$, 11.4~s at $N=512$). Together with binary, it is the only encoding that solves both $N=400$ and $N=512$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CPLEX CP is the fastest where it can run.} For $16\le N\le128$ it needs only 0.01--0.02~s, about 10--20 times faster than SAT (e.g.\ 0.02~s vs.\ 0.31~s at $N=128$). The \texttt{AllDifferent} propagator prunes far more per node than unit propagation on a flat CNF. (At $N=8$ its 1.31~s is most likely dominated by start-up overhead.)
  \item \textbf{Gurobi and CPLEX MIP} are also fast (mostly 0.01--0.04~s) up to their licence limits ($N=44$ and $N=31$). We cannot draw conclusions beyond these limits.
  \item \textbf{SAT scales further than CP-SAT.} CP-SAT (int) needs 16.1~s at $N=200$ and times out from $N=256$; CP-SAT (bool) reaches $N=256$ (3.5~s) but times out at $N=512$. SAT solves $N=512$ in 6.6~s and $N=1000$ in 27.7~s. Note that CP-SAT was run single-threaded; its default multi-threaded mode may behave very differently.
  \item \textbf{No exact method solves $N=2000$} within the limits: SAT and CP-SAT (bool) run out of memory.
\end{itemize}

\begin{finding}
\textbf{RQ3.} Within licence limits, CPLEX CP is the fastest exact method. Among methods that can run at every size, SAT (commander + MiniSat) scales furthest, reaching $N=1000$, about four times larger than single-threaded CP-SAT.
\end{finding}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Why clause count is not enough.}
Sequential counter and product produce the smallest CNFs, yet sequential is much slower than commander, and product is unstable across $N$. CDCL performance depends on how well unit propagation and clause learning work on the encoding's structure, not only on its size. Commander keeps clauses short and adds relatively few auxiliary variables, which seems to suit CDCL solvers well on this problem.

\paragraph{Why CP is fast.}
CP keeps the problem as three \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints over only $N$ integer variables, while SAT and ILP need $N^2$ variables. The global propagator reasons about a whole line at once, so much less search is needed.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Licences:} the SAT vs.\ Gurobi/CPLEX comparison is only valid for $N\le44$ and $N\le31$. With full licences these solvers may scale very differently.
  \item \emph{Single run, single core:} CDCL search has high variance between configurations, so small time differences are not statistically meaningful.
  \item \emph{Single thread:} CP-SAT and the commercial solvers are designed to use several threads; our setting is fair but not their best case.
  \item \emph{Scope:} we did not use symmetry-breaking clauses or more advanced cardinality encoders (e.g.\ PBLib).
\end{itemize}

%% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%% =====================================================================
We solved N-Queens with SAT using five hand-written and four PySAT AMO encodings on eight CDCL solvers, and compared SAT with CP-SAT, CPLEX CP, Gurobi and CPLEX MIP. All encodings were verified by exhaustive solution counting. Our main conclusions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Encoding (RQ1):} commander is the most robust AMO encoding; small CNF size does not guarantee fast solving.
  \item \textbf{Solvers (RQ2):} MiniSat and Glucose are the most robust solvers on this problem; they are the only ones to reach $N=1024$.
  \item \textbf{Paradigms (RQ3):} CPLEX CP is the fastest exact method within its licence ($N\le128$); SAT scales further than single-threaded CP-SAT and reaches $N=1000$.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Future work:} full commercial licences to extend the CP/ILP comparison to large $N$; symmetry-breaking SAT encodings and PBLib encoders; multi-threaded solvers; and repeated runs with several random seeds to measure variance.
